What is agentic AI?

You, a journalist, are interested in Bridgeport, CT.

A quick route to what’s happening locally is city council meetings, so we’ll ask ChatGPT what happened at the most recent meeting?

Instead of hallucinating, ChatGPT searches the web, finds the meeting minutes page, downloads the most recent minutes, and summarizes them for you. The act of “doing things” besides responding is what makes modern AI “agentic.”

With all the buzz about agents you would have expected a bit more, right? Don’t worry, we’ll get there!

Connecting external tools (Plugins)

PDFs and other text are “unstructured data” - a mishmash of text that takes effort to put into an organized format. One of the most valuable abilities of ChatGPT is to do that organizing work for us.

In this case, we’re interested in finding sources to interview. So instead of reading through the city council topics that were discussed, we’re interested in just the citizens who chose to speak. You can see how they’re separated out in the minutes PDF.

To organize it, we ask ChatGPT to take every person who spoke, their address, and provide a summary of their statement.

While it’s helpful to see the information presented in the chat, it would be more helpful if we were able to easily share it or observe the results over time. That probably means we want these results in an actual spreadsheet! Google Sheets is a nice simple approach.

To allow ChatGPT to read and write to Google Sheets, you need to use a plugin. Select Plugins from the left menu, then go through the flow to install Google Drive.

You might be nervous about giving so many permissions to OpenAI: unfortunately, you’re stuck! If you don’t give OpenAI everything, it sends you back to demand more! An alternative is to create a secondary account that you can grant access to.

To have your document saved to a Google Sheet, you simply say: save this to a Google Sheet. I’ve found that making use of multiple tabs in Sheets is very useful.

Despite asking ChatGPT to save to Google Sheets, it still might save it as an Excel file. To aggressively encourage it to use Sheets, Click the + next to where you type your prompt, then Plugin > Google Drive. Then you should be good (or close enough).

Now you have a spreadsheet!

Scheduling runs

Want it to run every day to catch new people and meetings? Just say: run every day at 7pm to update with new meetings and people.

You can also click “Scheduled” on the top left-hand corner of your ChatGPT interface.

This will work with sending emails, Slack messages, or anything else!

Getting AI’s opinions

One of the selling points of an AI agent is it has opinions and agency, so let’s do something a little more specific than summarization. Having AI put things into categories is useful – let’s say our beat is housing, how can we automatically flag speakers that might be interesting to us?

It’s easy! Just ask for another column to mark whether it is about housing or not.

You’ll magically have a whole new column! You might need to refine what you mean by housing, like whether parking minimums or zoning count, but if you spend time reviewing what is assigned to both categories you’ll be able to refine your prompt quickly.

Maybe there are a lot of people talking about housing, but you don’t care about most of them. You only care about a subset, or the specifically pressing, interesting ones. Since those don’t show up too often, though, you’re too busy to check the spreadsheet every day. What options do you have?

Part of being “agentic” is that the AI can decide which tools to use. It doesn’t always need to just update the spreadsheet, it can sometimes send Slack messages or sometimes send emails. So you say: send me an alert on Slack if anything really interesting shows up.

What’s “really interesting?” As always, you define it (or leave it up to AI to decide for you).

Custom skills and APIs

There’s a lot of data hiding out there about people and companies that might be useful in our spreadsheet. For example, some folks might have an undisclosed professional reason why they’re making a statement.

You can ask ChatGPT what information sources you can query with a name and an address, and it might suggest the Connecticut Business Registry. You can search by owner name and address, and use that to come up with all sorts of additional information.

You could have also done this the old fashioned way, by browsing around the Connecticut Open Data portal.

To add it as a data source on ChatGPT, you might start clicking around the Plugins section and end up weeping: “I wish there was a Plugin for the CT business registry! Doing this lookup manually is going to take far too much time!” You might also think about scraping, which is both 1) a pain and 2) doesn’t always work.

Fortunately, there’s an easy answer! With most government data, there’s a secret third option: an API. APIs are ways that two computers can talk to each other, in this case ChatGPT talking to the Connecticut state government portal. They’ll use Python code to shuffle data back and forth through the API.

Clicking around on the open data portal, we see the magic letters “API” and know we’re ready to go.

You don’t need to know Python or even how an API works, just that one exists: if you ask ChatGPT to learn how to use it, it can dig around for documentation and teach itself quickly. If you aren’t sure whether there is an API that covers a topic, ChatGPT can of course research for you: asking “find me an API about earthquakes” will give you a laundry list to try out.

Most APIs you’ll want to use again and again – I don’t want to look up one business one time, I want to look up eighty businesses every week. You don’t want ChatGPT to have to research the API each time, so we’ll tell ChatGPT to make a Skill for using the API. A skill is just like a plugin, except you customize how it works and can save and share them.

If you ask ChatGPT to turn the API into a skill, it (more or less) makes some notes and writes some code so that it always knows how to access the data. No need to have it burn tokens searching the internet and trying things out, you’ll just say “Search the business registry” and off it goes. Super convenient!

Once you’ve created a skill, going to the “Plugins” menu and then the “Skills” tab will reveal it.

These skills are portable: once you’ve made them once you can use them in any conversation! Depending on how picky ChatGPT is acting you might need to go through the Plugins > Skills menu first to select the skill, but it will often work without that.

Even though I originally created the skill in the conversation about meeting minutes, below you’ll see I can search for businesses at an address in Connecticut in a brand new conversation.

Found you!

Adding special APIs

APIs are ways that two computers talk to each other, but sometimes they need to prove who they’re working for. Sometimes this is because information is sensitive, or sometimes it’s because they’d like to charge you. Instead of a username and password, APIs use an api key.

In our investigations, we’re now interested in looking up political campaign contributions. We might ask ChatGPT where to find a campaign contribution API, and it would point us to OpenFEC from the FEC.

While we don’t understand many of the words on the page, we can read two things: they have an API for FEC data, and you need to register to get an API key. Again: we still don’t really know what an API is, we just know it’s a way information can easily be shared with ChatGPT!

After you register you get a long string of text in your email inbox. This is a very typical API key! No username/password combo: you’ll just provide the key to prove who you are when ChatGPT talks to the API for you.

Just like we did with the last API skill, we’ll tell ChatGPT to help us build it.

In this case I hedge a little and ask whether it needs anything – I know it needs an API key! Instead of asking me outright, it tries to use a demo key that it found in the documentation. (Bzzt! Not what I wanted.)

That works, but I’d prefer to use mine.

I paste the API key into the chat and ChatGPT immediately gets very very concerned about privacy. ChatGPT is overly worried about protecting API keys: “I can’t save it! You should keep it somewhere else!” But I promise it’s as private as anything else in your conversation. Just like I reassured you, you can reassure ChatGPT, and then it will be happy to save it for you.

After you’ve added your API key you’re free to tell ChatGPT to flesh out the spreadsheet even more, with any sort of campaign contributions it can find.

Now that you know about APIs and API keys, you can add almost any public data to your spreadsheet: liquor licenses, restaurant inspections, etc, almost always have APIs that ChatGPT can build on. Also, because the AI is “agentic” it can decide what searches to perform - if it’s a law office it won’t look up OSHA violations, but it might for a construction site.

Making it local

Maybe you have a collection of documents on your machine - for now, let’s say nice clean PDFs - and you want ChatGPT to search through them in the same way you search through the minutes. You want a spreadsheet, you want the columns, you want the research.

You can test this out by using your own documents, or downloading a few of the meeting minutes or agendas to your own computer.

So you download the local version of ChatGPT, click the “Work” tab, and create a new project. Add your folder of PDFs as a “Source folder” that ChatGPT can have access to.

You’re all ready to say “research the people in these PDFs,” but if you open up Plugins > Skills, you’ll find your fancy new skills aren’t there!

Web and Desktop skills are NOT shared. To get your skills moved to the desktop, you need to download them from the web and then install them on your desktop ChatGPT.

First, download: Plugins > Skills on the web, then … and Download. This will download a zip file.

To add them to Desktop ChatGPT, you drag them into the chat window and say “add this as a skill.” For some reason, it does not work if you go through Plugins > Add. You have to do it through a conversation! You can also choose to add it as a skill for all chats or just the ones in an individual project.

Just like on the web, it will panic about adding an API key anywhere it deems is “unsafe.” I promise you your computer is reasonably secure, so you can promise ChatGPT that it’ll be okay.

Now you can use the skills just like you can on the web! Feel free to set it up to analyze every time you drop a new file in the folder.

Il’l note that in my first run ChatGPT went wild on doing secondary research, so I had to add another line to the chat telling it to calm down. That’s a good reminder to me that in my original prompt I should have said “only use the skills, don’t search the web!”

This time I was looking for a spreadsheet on my computer instead of one in Google Sheets. Unlike software that always does the same thing every time, the beauty of AI is that it is so flexible in regards to what you ask it to do. It decides which tools and skills to use, which allows you to glue small pieces together to make a more interesting larger picture.

Extending your process

In this series we’ve learned how to use AI agents to connect to external services, schedule tasks, create personal skills, leverage APIs, and use them both on the web and on our own computer.

For your next step, I’d recommend building a tip processing pipeline. It should take an email with a name, email address and body, and then determine whether the tip is worth pursuing or not.

In our example above, most of the process was step-by-step. You always wanted to look up businesses, you always wanted to get OpenFEC data, etc etc You only ever had an optional piece where you said “ping me on Slack for important things!”

One of the strengths of agentic AI is its ability to make decisions. For example, you could write a prompt like this:

What do you think would work? What wouldn’t? What do you need to connect it to in order to get the necessary information, or is web search enough?

Avoiding hallucinations and saving tokens

One of the big gotchas about ChatGPT and other LLMs is that despite their best efforts, they’ll always hallucinate. Even if you give it a document – like the minutes – and ask it to recite what it’s read verbatim, it still might get tripped up.

One way around this is to be specific about how you want the data extracted: write code to extract the names and addresses of the citizen speakers. Instead of doing its best to copy through its big AI brain, ChatGPT will instead write code that takes into account indentation, address formatting, headers, font sizes and styles, and other clues to automatically pull out what you’re looking for.

This also saves ChatGPT credits down the line because instead of ChatGPT reading the PDF page by page, you’re running Python code that does the extraction on its behalf. You’re still relying on ChatGPT for the summarization, categorizing (housing/not housing), or doing web research or Slack notifications, but some of the weight is lifted off its shoulders.


A potential gotcha in our case is that those PDFs are… not very pretty. There’s a good chance someone is manually assembling them, and with a little error or change in formatting down the line we’ll stop having successful updates! So our job is to weigh the potential for errors (and running out of credits) versus flexibility and not babysitting the process.

It’s up to you, but it’s always worth giving a try!
